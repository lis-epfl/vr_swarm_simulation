What the run covered. 63 buildable city designs (block spacing 40–200 m × coverage 0.02–0.14, 12 m streets), 627 cities, 14 five-minute random walks per city flown by the single drone and the swarm at spreads 0.4, 1.0 and 1.6 (8,778 walks, about 2,900 flight-hours), and 6 standing points per city. No job failed, and 176 re-flown walks matched their first flight exactly. Every interval and p value below treats the cities as the independent units.

1. The swarm is ahead everywhere. On all eleven swarm ÷ single measures, in all 63 designs, at every spread, the swarm is ahead with q < 0.05 (2,079 of 2,079 squares).

2. What each sees per metre hardly depends on the city. At spread 1.0 the swarm clears 1.93× the single drone's sweep width (median over designs; range 1.87–2.08; SD of the city ratios 0.035), and 1.44× (1.34–1.49) counting only the screens in the headset's view. Coverage moves it by +0.6% per 0.01 (p < 0.0001, but tiny). Standing still the swarm shows 6.3× the ground on screen and 1.8× in the headset's view, also nearly flat. Found after the run: this sweep width only counts ground within 42 m of the track, which the swarm nearly fills (82.5 m of an 84 m maximum), so the swarm's ratio is understated by about 6–7% at spread 1.0 and more at 1.6, and it cannot rank 1.6 against 1.0. The code now measures to 80 m either side; these numbers need re-flying to be corrected. Ground per metre (item 4) has no such ceiling.

3. Finding a hidden target: the advantage grows with building density, and block size barely matters. The swarm's speed-up in finding a target hidden anywhere in the city (200 m, any shown feed, spread 1.0) is 1.48× at coverage 0.02, 1.92× at 0.06, 2.58× at 0.10 and 3.5× at 0.14. The trend is +7.9% per 0.01 of coverage (p < 0.0001, R² 0.91 over 627 cities) and −4.7% per doubling of the block spacing (p < 0.0001). Counting only the screens in the headset's view, it is 1.19×, 1.30×, 1.51× and 1.96×. About half of the growth is speed: the single drone, steering round every building, slows from 6.2 to 1.8 m/s as coverage rises, while the swarm, flowing between buildings, slows from 6.1 to about 2.5–3.2 m/s. The random walker's mean time to find the target grows from 7.6 to 35 minutes with the single drone and from 5.2 to 12.6 minutes with the swarm, between coverage 0.02 and 0.10.

4. Wider is better, with one limit. Against spread 1.0, spread 1.6 covers ground 11% faster (better in 62 of 63 designs), finds a hidden target 4% sooner (51 better, 3 worse), sweeps 5% more in the headset's view (43 better, 4 worse) and loses fewest drones (0.26 per walk against 0.88). Spread 0.4 is worse than 1.0 on every measure in all 63 designs (6–13%) and loses 4.8 drones per walk; in the densest designs more than half of its walks end with fewer than four drones.

5. The limit: neighbouring screens stop sharing ground. Flying, neighbouring feeds show some of the same ground 49%, 42% and 26% of the time at spreads 0.4, 1.0 and 1.6. At 1.0 that falls from 65% at coverage 0.02 to 30% at 0.12; at 1.6, from 44% to 13–15%. An unbroken ring of screens is rare: 11%, 3% and 0.1% of the time. It costs the wide formation coverage. In cities where widening from 1.0 to 1.6 lost more of that overlap, the swarm gained less from widening, with the design held fixed: partial r = −0.42 for sweep width and for ground per second (p < 0.0001, 627 cities). In small, dense blocks (40–75 m at coverage 0.08–0.12), where the 24 m formation is twice the street width, 1.6 is 3–9% worse than 1.0.

Read with three caveats. These come from the 2-D replica, not yet checked in Unity in these mixed-building cities. The walker is task-free: in the search study, people found goals 2.3× (swarm) and 10× (single drone) faster than the model, so real pilots' ratios will be smaller. And discovery at 200 m is an assumption; at 40 m the swarm's speed-up is similar or larger.
